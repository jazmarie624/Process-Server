Tricks of the trade

None of this is in the statute or the class. It's the difference between 5 serves out of 8 stops and 2.

Read the house before you knock

Tap each marker. Ten seconds of looking saves a whole return trip — and every one of these becomes a line in your attempt log.

Tap a number

Each marker is something you should clock before your knuckles hit the door.

Getting the door to open

Look like a delivery

Papers folded or in a plain envelope, held low at your side. A legal-size stack visible through a peephole is why doors don't open.

Knock in threes, then step back

Three firm knocks, then one step back and look relaxed. Pressed against the door reads as police. Stepping back reads as harmless.

Ring AND knock

Doorbells get ignored, knocks get investigated. Both together reads as "someone with a package."

Park down the street

An unfamiliar car out front tips them off before you reach the door. Two houses down, then walk.

Never block the driveway

It announces you, it traps you, and it starts the conversation with them already annoyed.

Speak first, fast

The second the door cracks, use their name warmly. Hesitation reads as threat; confidence reads as routine.

When they're dodging

The wrong-name test

Ask for a made-up person: "Is Robert here?" A dodger who yells "wrong house, this is the Ramirez place" just confirmed the address without meaning to.

Change the vehicle

If they've clocked your car, borrow a different one for attempt three. Costs nothing, works constantly.

Knock next door first

Neighbors will give you the schedule — "she works nights, home around 8" — if you're just looking for someone. Never mention papers.

Go to work instead

People who dodge at home rarely dodge at work, and Florida law requires the employer to give you a private area. §48.031(1)(b)

The car is the tell

Same plate at the address on three separate attempts is the strongest fact in a diligent-search affidavit. Photograph it every time.

Stop guessing, start charging

After 3–4 documented attempts, quote a skip trace or a stakeout. Free trips out of pride are unpaid work, not diligence.

Apartments, gates, and complexes

Leasing offices won't confirm — but they'll correct

"I have a delivery for Ramirez in 214" often gets "she's in 412." Let them fix your wrong information.

Maintenance knows everything

They know who actually lives where and whose car is whose — and they're not trained to stonewall.

Read the callbox directory

Names in the gate directory confirm residency for free, before you spend an attempt.

Gate guards: paper beats argument

Hand them the printed statute instead of debating. Still refused? Get the guard's name, company, and time, and tell the attorney. §48.031(7)

Stack the complex

Before driving to any large complex, check every open job for that same community. One trip, three serves.

Catch the mail carrier

Carriers know who's still receiving mail there. Ask casually mid-route.

Speed tricks — where the hours actually go

  1. Pre-fill the return before you leave the house. Everything except date, time, and manner. Forty seconds instead of six minutes.
  2. Stamp every packet the night before. Name and CPS number pre-stamped; only date, time, and initials get handwritten at the door.
  3. Dictate, don't type. Voice-memo your observations the second you're back in the car. Details evaporate in ten minutes.
  4. Sticky note on each packet with description, vehicle, and best time. You won't remember which defendant is which by stop five.
  5. Highlight the deadline on the outside of the packet, where you see it while flipping — not buried inside.
  6. Carry two copies of everything. Papers get torn, dropped, rained on, thrown back. A second set saves the trip.
  7. Do the return in the car. The 15 minutes you "save" becomes an hour of reconstructing your day at 10 PM.
  8. Never leave a zone half-finished to chase a rush across town. Crossing 192 at 5 PM costs you a serve.

Client tricks — this is how you get called again

Text the moment it's served

"Ramirez served personally, 8/14 6:42 AM, return tonight." Attorneys forgive a slow serve. They don't forgive silence.

Report failure early and loud

Two failed attempts with a deadline five days out? Email tonight. A client blindsided on the due date never calls back.

Invoice the same day

Attach the return. Paid returns get filed faster and remembered better.

Ask "when do you need it by"

Not "is this a rush" — everyone says no to that. A real date lets you price and sequence it right.

Lead with the Spanish

In Osceola County, "I serve in Spanish" wins work over servers with ten years on you. First line of your intro, not the last.

Be the 4 PM person

Whoever takes a rush at 4 PM on a Friday owns that firm's business. Worth more than any rate cut.

Florida gear

Cooler in the trunk

Water and a cold towel. Heat exhaustion ends the day.

Closed shoes, always

Fire ants, glass, dog runs. Never sandals.

Battery pack

Your phone is GPS, camera, timestamp, and evidence.

Gallon bags

Wet papers can't be served. Bag every packet.

Cash in the console

Gates, lots, situations. $20 solves things.

Two phone mounts

One navigating, one recording. Fumbling looks unsafe.

Printed §48.031(7)

For gate guards. Hand it over instead of arguing.

Hardest address first

Do the scary one while you're fresh. Fear compounds if you save it.

The five habits that protect your certification

  • Never sign a return you're unsure about. A willfully false return is a third-degree felony and a permanent statewide ban. A re-attempt costs $20. §48.31(2)
  • Never serve on a Sunday. Void service, and you're personally liable for damages as if you had no process at all. §48.20
  • Never serve a case you have a stake in. You must be disinterested.
  • Never imply you're law enforcement. It's a crime and it voids the serve you just worked for.
  • Never let the bond or ID card lapse. An expired card is something opposing counsel can attack.

Set up shop

Build the system once, before your first job, and you never lose a paper. Build it while you're already busy and you will.

The car build — tap a zone

Your car is your office. Every item has one home, and the back seat stays empty.

Tap a number

Eight zones. If an item doesn't have a zone, it doesn't come in the car.

The three-bin rule — why you'll never lose a paper

Every document in your possession is in exactly one of three places. There is no fourth place. Nothing loose, ever.

1 · INBOX

New jobs, not yet processed. A single folder on the passenger seat. Emptied every night — nothing sleeps in the inbox.

→
2 · ACTIVE

The accordion file in the passenger footwell. One slot per open job, tabbed by due date, not client. Front slot = due this week.

→
3 · CLOSED

A box at home, filed by month. Signed return, invoice, attempt log. Kept 7 years to match your taxes.

The test: pick up any piece of paper in your car right now. If you can't say which bin it belongs to in two seconds, your system has already broken. Fix it that night, not next week.

Day one — build this before your first job

  1. Separate business checking account and debit card. Every serve fee lands here. Every gas fill and supply run comes out of here. Mixing this with household money is the mistake that makes taxes impossible.
  2. One cloud folder tree. Google Drive or Dropbox, mirrored to your iPad, with an exact naming convention you never break:
/ServeBook /Active 2026SC004578_RAMIREZ/ 01_documents.pdf 02_attempt_2026-08-14_0642.jpg 03_return_signed.pdf 04_invoice.pdf /Closed /2026-08/ /Templates return_template.pdf invoice_template.pdf intake_form.pdf statute_48-031-7.pdf
Case number first, then last name. Always. It sorts chronologically, it matches what the attorney says on the phone, and it means you can find any file in four seconds from the driver's seat.
  1. A dedicated work email with a signature block: your name, "Certified Process Server, Ninth Judicial Circuit," your CPS number, phone. That number in your signature is what makes attorneys take you seriously.
  2. Your master board. One spreadsheet, one row per job. Columns: Case # · Defendant · Address · Client · Received date · Serve-by date · Attempt 1 · Attempt 2 · Attempt 3 · Status · Fee · Invoiced · Paid. Sort by serve-by date. This single sheet is your whole business until volume forces software.
  3. Two calendar alarm types. A 5-day warning and a 2-day warning on every serve-by date, set the moment you accept the job. Deadlines don't sneak up on people who set both.
  4. Phone setup. Contacts for attorneys prefixed ATTY — so they sort together. A photo album per case. Timestamp camera app installed and permissions granted before you're standing at a door.
  5. Templates built and saved. Return of service, invoice, intake form, and a diligent-search affidavit. Build them once from your mentor's versions.
  6. Mileage app on automatic tracking. Turn it on day one and forget it.
  7. Self-inking stamp ordered. Your name and CPS number. Everything else at the door is handwritten.

The rhythm — what a working day actually looks like

Night before

Print and stamp packets. Sticky note each with description, vehicle, best time. Build the route. Load the accordion in due-date order.

6:00–9:00 AM

Prime block. 6–8 stops, hardest defendant first. Return written in the car after each serve.

Midday

Skip tracing, calls to clients, invoicing, new intake. This is desk time, not driving time.

6:00–9:00 PM

Second block. Different addresses than the morning, or the same ones at a different hour.

Then the 15-minute close-out, every single night: log attempts, upload photos, send returns, flag anything at risk, rebuild tomorrow's route, log miles. The servers who quit are the ones who skipped this for two weeks and then couldn't dig out.

The weekly reset — Sunday, 30 minutes

You can't serve on Sunday anyway. Use it.

Purge the accordion

Anything served and returned moves to the closed box. The accordion should only ever hold live work.

Chase the money

Scan the master board for anything invoiced over 30 days. One polite email each. This is how solos avoid getting stiffed.

Look at next week

Any serve-by date inside 7 days with zero attempts logged gets scheduled into Monday's prime block right now.

What "successful" looks like at 30, 90, and 365 days

MarkWhat you should have
30 daysSystem built. First solo serves done. One return your mentor would sign. Two clients who've used you more than once. Mileage and a separate account running from day one.

The one number that matters: percentage served within the client's deadline. Not serves per week, not revenue. Attorneys hire on that number and nothing else, and it's the number your reputation in a circuit this size is built on.

Florida Chapter 48 — the working cheat sheet

This is what separates a $65 serve from a motion to quash.

Substitute service — tap all three prongs

Courts require strict compliance and construe it strictly against you. All three or nothing.

PRONG 1
Usual place of abode
Where they actually live right now. Not a mailing address, not the license address, not last month's apartment.
PRONG 2
Resident, 15 or older
Must live there. A visiting mother-in-law, babysitter, or contractor is not a resident. Ask, and record the answer.
PRONG 3
Informed of contents
A spoken act you actually perform and then swear to. Say it out loud, every single time.
SERVICE FAILSTap the prongs you completed.
§48.031(1)(a)  Personal service is simpler: hand the process plus the complaint or initial pleading directly to the named person. Done.

Evictions — your highest-volume work

Posting a residential eviction summons

If the tenant can't be found in the county, or nobody 15+ resides there, post the summons conspicuously on the property — but only after two attempts at least 6 hours apart.

Log both times to the minute. That 6-hour gap is the single most-challenged fact in eviction service. §48.183(1)

Unknown parties in possession

Three attempts, spread across: once during business hours, once during non-business hours, once on a weekend. You must also ask the occupant's name and put it on the return — or state that you asked and couldn't get it. §48.184(3)

What a judge means by "due diligence"

You've used due diligence when you made diligent inquiry, made an honest and conscientious effort appropriate to the circumstances, used the knowledge reasonably available to you, and made an appropriate number of attempts at times and places the person is reasonably likely to be found.

In plain terms: varied days, varied times, documented, plus at least one records check. Three attempts at 2 PM on weekdays is not diligence, and an attorney will tell you so. §48.161(5)

The knock — every scene, English and Spanish

Practice out loud in the car. Turn on Drill mode to hide the Spanish and test yourself.

Before you knock — the 10-second reset

Papers in your non-dominant hand. ID clipped and visible. Phone recording or timestamping. Stand to the side of the door, never centered. Read the name once more so you say it right the first time. Then three firm knocks.

Scene 1 · Correct person answers

You — opening
Good morning. Are you Maria Ramirez?
Buenos días. ¿Es usted María Ramírez?
You — the handoff
I have legal documents for you from the Osceola County court. These are for you.
Tengo documentos legales para usted del tribunal del condado de Osceola. Estos son para usted.
You — the close
You've been served. The papers explain what this is about and the deadline to respond. Have a good day.
Ha sido notificada oficialmente. Los papeles explican de qué se trata y la fecha límite para responder. Que tenga buen día.
Then leave. Don't linger, don't explain, don't answer legal questions. Note the exact time before you reach your car — memory degrades within minutes.

Scene 2 · Someone else answers — building substitute service

You — confirm the abode
Hi — does Maria Ramirez live here?
Hola — ¿vive aquí María Ramírez?
You — confirm THEY reside there (the prong people miss)
And do you live here too? Are you 15 or older?
¿Y usted también vive aquí? ¿Tiene 15 años o más?
You — inform of contents, out loud, always
I'm leaving these with you. They are legal documents for Maria — a summons and complaint from the court. Please give them to her.
Le voy a dejar estos documentos. Son documentos legales para María — una citación y una demanda del tribunal. Por favor entrégueselos a ella.
You — get the name for your return
Can I get your first name and your relationship to her? It goes on the court's form.
¿Me puede dar su nombre y su relación con ella? Es para el formulario del tribunal.
If they say they don't live there — stop. Do not leave the papers. Log it as an attempt with who you spoke to and what they said.

Scene 3 · "What is this about?"

You
I'm not able to give legal advice and I don't know the details of the case. The first page has the case number and the court. The attorney's name and phone number are on the front, or you can call the clerk of court.
No puedo dar consejos legales y no conozco los detalles del caso. La primera página tiene el número del caso y el tribunal. El nombre y el teléfono del abogado están en la primera página, o puede llamar a la secretaría del tribunal.
Never speculate. "It looks like a credit card thing" is legal advice from a stranger, and it will end up in a deposition.

Scene 4 · They refuse to take the papers

You — after identity is confirmed
That's completely fine. I'm placing them right here. You have been served.
Está bien, no hay problema. Los dejo aquí mismo. Usted ha sido notificado oficialmente.
The rule: once you've identified the right person and told them what the papers are, refusal doesn't defeat service. Set them down in near proximity — at their feet, on the threshold, in the door — and write in your return that you identified them, informed them, they refused, and you left the documents in their presence. Never do this without confirmed identity.

Scene 5 · They won't open but you can hear them

You — through the door
Miss Ramirez, I'm a certified process server. I have court documents for you. I'm going to leave them at your door.
Señora Ramírez, soy servidora de procesos certificada. Tengo documentos del tribunal para usted. Los voy a dejar en su puerta.
Careful. A voice through a door is usually not confirmed identity. Unless you saw them or they answered to their name and you can swear to who they are, log this as an attempt, not a serve, and come back at a different hour. This is exactly where new servers get burned.

Scene 6 · Gate guard or front desk

You
Good morning. I'm a certified process server for the Ninth Judicial Circuit — here's my ID and my server number. Florida Statute 48.031(7) requires a gated community to grant unannounced entry for service of process. I'm not asking you to call ahead. I just need the gate.
Buenos días. Soy servidora de procesos certificada del Noveno Circuito Judicial — aquí está mi identificación y mi número. El Estatuto de Florida 48.031(7) requiere que una comunidad con portón permita la entrada sin anunciar para la entrega de documentos legales. No le pido que llame. Solo necesito que abra el portón.
Keep §48.031(7) printed in your door bag. Hand it over instead of arguing. Still refused? Get the guard's name, company, and time, then let the attorney handle it. Never force a gate.

Scene 7 · Serving at a workplace

You — to the manager
Hi, I'm a certified process server. I need to serve one of your employees with legal documents. Florida law requires you to let me do that in a private area — and I'd rather do it discreetly than in front of customers. Where would you like to put us?
Hola, soy servidora de procesos certificada. Necesito entregarle documentos legales a uno de sus empleados. La ley de Florida requiere que me permita hacerlo en un área privada — y prefiero hacerlo con discreción, no frente a los clientes. ¿Dónde prefiere que lo hagamos?
Framing it as protecting them from a scene gets you a back office almost every time. The statute is your backup, not your opener.

Scene 8 · Hostile or yelling

You — flat, calm, already stepping back
I'm not with the police and I'm not here to argue. I'm a certified process server. I don't know anything about your case — I just deliver the documents. Everything you need is inside. Have a good day.
No soy policía y no vine a discutir. Soy servidora de procesos certificada. No sé nada de su caso — solo entrego los documentos. Toda la información está adentro. Que tenga buen día.
Say it once, then leave. Walk backward two steps, turn, go. You don't get a second serve if you're injured.

Scene 9 · Confirming identity when they're evasive

The wrong-detail method
I have a delivery for a Maria Ramirez with a middle initial J — is that you, or do I have the wrong unit?
Tengo una entrega para María Ramírez con la inicial J — ¿es usted, o tengo la unidad equivocada?
The spelling method
Can you spell the last name for me so I mark this correctly?
¿Me puede deletrear el apellido para marcarlo correctamente?
The line you don't cross: confirming identity is fine. Pretending to be UPS, a florist, a cop, or a utility worker is not — it can void your service and cost your certification, and impersonating law enforcement is a crime. Be creative about timing, not about who you are.
Spanish notes for practice
  • Notificado / notificada — "no-tee-fee-CAH-do." Use -a for a woman, -o for a man. This is the word that makes you sound like you belong.
  • Citación = summons · demanda = complaint · tribunal or corte = court · fecha límite = deadline.
  • Stay in usted, never tú. Formality keeps it professional and protects you.
  • Osceola is heavily Puerto Rican — "Que tenga buen día" lands warmer than anything from a translation app.
  • Run out of Spanish mid-conversation? "Disculpe, mi español es limitado — toda la información está en los papeles." Honest beats fluent.

Timing & routes

Serving is 20% knocking and 80% deciding when — and in what order — to knock.

The week at a glance

Prime — hit your hardest defendants here Good Low yield Service is void
Sunday is blacked out for a reason. Service or execution on Sunday is void and you're personally liable to the aggrieved party for damages, as if you had no process at all. You can drive by on Sunday to scout — photograph tags, confirm someone lives there — then serve Monday at 6:30 AM. §48.20

The attempt ladder

#WhenWhy it works
1Weekday 6:15–7:45 AMHighest hit rate in Florida. Nobody dodges before coffee and the cars are still in the driveway. Spend it on your hardest defendant, not your easiest.
2Weekday 7–9 PMSecond best. Different day of the week than attempt 1. Lights on means someone's home.
3Saturday 9 AM–1 PMCatches shift workers and weekend-only residents. Required for unknown-occupant evictions anyway.

Bonus windows: trash-collection morning (people come outside without checking the peephole) · right after a hard afternoon rain (everyone's home) · Friday 5–8 PM · the 1st and the 15th.

Record this at every attempt

Time

To the minute. Not "morning."

GPS

Auto-captured. Verify it landed.

Photo

House number and front door, timestamped.

Observations

Vehicles and tags, lights, mail, dogs, toys, AC running, blinds moving.

Those observations become a diligent-search affidavit that survives a challenge — and they're what an attorney remembers when deciding who to call next. "No answer" three times is a return that gets your invoice questioned.

Route building — the 90-minute block

  1. Batch by geography, not by client. Osceola splits into St. Cloud/Narcoossee, Kissimmee core (192), Poinciana, Buenaventura Lakes, Celebration/Four Corners. Orange splits into downtown/Parramore, east (UCF/Alafaya), south (Hunter's Creek/Meadow Woods), west (Ocoee/Winter Garden), north (Apopka).
  2. Don't cross the 192 corridor at rush hour. Finish a zone before you leave it. One wasted crossing is 25 minutes and half a serve.
  3. Anchor on rush jobs. Deadline jobs come first in the sequence, not first on the map.
  4. Cap at 6–8 stops per block. More than that and you rush knocks, which is when returns get sloppy.
  5. Sequence within a zone: gated communities early (guard shift changes are friendlier), apartments mid-block, single-family last.
  6. Leave one flex slot. Something always takes 40 minutes instead of 8.
  7. Log the return in the car, before you pull off.

Free first
  • Osceola / Orange property appraiser
  • Clerk of court case search — other cases, other addresses
  • Sunbiz.org — businesses, agents, officer addresses
  • Florida voter registration lookup
  • Facebook / Instagram — employer, neighborhood, cars in photos
Paid, when it's worth it
  • TLOxp or IRBsearch — industry standard, requires credentialing
  • Tracers or Delvepoint — cheaper entry for solos
  • Charge a skip-trace fee. Don't eat it.
The neighbor knock

"Hi — I'm trying to reach the Ramirez family, do they still live in that unit?" Do not say you're serving papers. You're allowed to look for someone; you're not allowed to broadcast their litigation to the block.

The paper system

One rule above all: a document never sits loose. It's in a case folder, in the accordion, or in the client's hands.

Intake checklist — run this the second a job comes in

Two returns, same serve

SERVED
On 8/14/2026 at 6:42 AM, I served Maria J. Ramirez by leaving a copy of the Summons and Complaint at 1428 Blossom Ct, St. Cloud, FL 34769, her usual place of abode, with Carlos Ramirez, her adult son, age 24, who resides therein, and informed him of the contents thereof.
Why it holds: abode named, resident named, age stated, residency stated, contents informed, documents listed, date and time exact.
QUASHED
Served at the residence with a family member.
Four fatal gaps in nine words: which family member? Did they reside there? How old were they? Were they informed of the contents? And was it actually the abode?

Required fields on every return

  • Date and time the process came to hand — when you received it. People forget this one constantly.
  • Date and time it was served
  • Manner of service — personal, substitute, posting, corporate
  • Name of the person served
  • Their position or capacity if served in a representative role
  • A list of every pleading and document served
  • Your signature — electronic is permitted

Missing facts or a missing signature invalidates the service — but the return is amendable on application to the court. Fix it fast and don't hide it. §48.21

Separately: on the first page of at least one process, write the date, the time, your initials or signature, and your ID number. That's statutory, not a formality. §48.031(5) · §48.29(6)

Filing — three layers, that's it

1 · The car (active)

One accordion file, one slot per open job, tabbed by due date — not by client. Front slot is due this week. Served jobs leave the accordion that same day.

2 · The phone (evidence)

Photos and GPS auto-upload to CASE#_LASTNAME the same day. Phones die and get stolen.

3 · The box (closed)

Signed return, invoice, and attempt log, filed by month. Keep 7 years to match your tax records.

The daily close-out — 15 minutes, every working day

  1. Log every attempt with times. Even the no-answers.
  2. Upload photos to the right case folders.
  3. Draft, sign, and send returns for anything served today.
  4. Flag anything at risk — a deadline inside 5 days with no successful attempt gets an email tonight.
  5. Rebuild tomorrow's route from what's left.
  6. Log your miles.
This close-out is the job. Servers lose clients not because they can't serve, but because attorneys can't get a status update.

Getting clients — in the order that works

  1. Your mentor's overflow. That's your entire first month. Be the person he can hand a rush to at 4 PM and stop thinking about.
  2. Proof and ABC Legal. Fills dead space in routes you're already driving. Low pay, zero sales effort.
  3. Eviction firms and property managers. Osceola and Orange both run high eviction volume, and it's recurring. One good property management company is a steady weekly base. Go get two.
  4. Small family-law and collections firms. One-page intro email with your CPS number and your turnaround promise, then follow up in person. Solo attorneys respond to faces.
  5. Bilingual as the differentiator. In Osceola County, "serves in Spanish" is a real competitive edge. First line, not last.

Taxes — set this up now, not in April

You're 1099

Schedule C self-employment. Clients issue 1099-NEC over $600. Nobody withholds anything for you.

Set aside 25–30%

Every payment, the day it lands, moves to a separate account. This one habit keeps gig income from becoming a tax bill.

Deduct

Mileage (check the current IRS standard rate), bond, certification and renewal fees, software, phone %, printing, supplies.

Quarterly estimated payments may apply once this income is meaningful. Worth an hour with a tax preparer before your first quarter closes — I'm not a tax professional and the thresholds shift.

Safety

You're handing strangers the worst news of their week, alone, at their home. Take it seriously without being afraid of it.

The approach — muscle memory

Park for exit

Nose out. Never blocked in a driveway, never behind another car in a narrow lot.

Stand off-center

Never directly in front of the door. Hinge side, one step back.

Hands free

Papers in the non-dominant hand. Dominant hand empty. Nothing in your pockets.

Scan first

Dogs, cameras, open garage, people sitting in cars, second exit.

Knock, don't pound

A hammering fist reads as police and puts people on defense before the door opens.

Tell someone

Share your route with your partner or your oldest. Live location on, check-in text when the block is done.

De-escalation, in order

  1. Lower your voice. People match volume. Going quieter pulls them down with you.
  2. Drop the "I." "The court is requiring this" beats "I'm serving you." You're the mail carrier, not the sender.
  3. Give them the out. "The attorney's number is on the front page." People calm down when handed a next step.
  4. Don't defend, explain, or correct. You will not win a legal argument in a doorway and you don't get paid to.
  5. Leave while it's still calm. The best moment to exit is one beat before you think you need to.

Hard rules

Never
  • Enter a home, even if invited, even if it's raining
  • Go past a fence or into a backyard
  • Open an unlocked gate with a dog behind it
  • Argue, follow someone, or chase a car
  • Return alone to an address that already went bad
  • Serve with your kids in the car
  • Say or imply you're law enforcement
Always
  • ID visible before the door opens
  • Phone recording or timestamping
  • Doors locked the second you're back in the car
  • Drive one block before you write anything down
  • Report a threat to the client and flag the address in your system
  • Call 911 and file a report if you're threatened — that report protects the case and you

Dogs

A loose dog in the yard means no attempt. Log it: "Attempt aborted — unsecured dog in front yard." That's a legitimate, billable, documented attempt. Nobody expects you to fight a dog for $65. Keep the gate between you, and if a dog charges, stand still and turn sideways rather than running — running triggers chase drive.

On carrying

Plenty of Florida servers carry; plenty don't. If you go that route, get the license properly and understand that a visible weapon changes every doorway interaction you have, and that a serve is never worth a use-of-force decision. Some client contracts and some properties prohibit it outright. Ask your mentor what he does and why — before your first hostile door, not after.

Week one with your mentor

Show up with these written down. Nothing makes a mentor invest in you faster than realizing you did homework.

Ask him these, in this order

  1. Can I see three of your returns of service? Two good, one that got challenged. Copy his format exactly. Highest-value thing you can get from him.
  2. What's your rate sheet, and what do you charge for a non-serve? You need the real local number, not a range from the internet.
  3. Which attorneys and property managers in Osceola actually pay on time? And who should I not take work from?
  4. Who files the return — you or the attorney?
  5. Walk me through the last serve you had quashed. Failures teach faster than successes.
  6. What's your intake form? Ask for a copy.
  7. Can I ride along for a full morning block before I run solo? Watch how he opens and how fast he leaves.
  8. Will you take my overflow calls the first month? Ask directly. Worst answer is no.
  9. What does the Ninth Circuit specifically care about at renewal? Circuit habits aren't in the statute.
  10. How do you handle a defendant who's clearly dodging? Get his real playbook, not the textbook one.

Your first-30-days checklist

How you'll know week one went well

You served someone

Even one. The first serve breaks the fear and everything after it is technique.

You wrote a return he'd sign

If your return matches his format, you're employable to any attorney in the circuit.

You have a next job

One scheduled serve for next week means the pipeline started. That's the whole goal.
